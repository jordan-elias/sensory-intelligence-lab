Sensory Intelligence Lab

Experience, explore, regulate through sound.

A laboratory for sensory intelligence. Discover how sound shapes your attention, emotion, and state. Learn from experimental composers. Build your toolkit for calm, focus, and creative exploration. Create an account for free.

Regulation Engine — generative soundscapes that meet your current state and guide you toward calm, focus, or activation using the ISO principle.
Breath Regulation — visual and sonic guides for four evidence-based breathing patterns including box breathing and the physiological sigh.
Journaling & Check-ins — explore emotional tracking and reflective writing to enhance your self-awareness and personal growth.
Ear Training & Spatial Awareness — perceptual skill-building for auditory attention, sound localisation, and scene analysis.
Compositional tools — learn about experimental composers and try their techniques yourself through guided experiments.

Created by Jordan Elias, music therapist based in Berlin. Modules are grounded in music therapy and neuroscience research.

About this project

What this is and why I built it

I've been a music therapist since 2017. In that time I've worked with people one-to-one, in groups, in clinics and over video call. And while there have been so many moments of connection, clarity, and growth, I've been sitting with the question: what happens to the work between sessions?

A single therapy session carries a lot of pressure and expectation. You arrive, you want to make the most of your time, you open something up, and then you go back to your life and try to hold it until next time. A lot of times, that gap is where the work gets lost. The insight fades and you fall back into your patterns. The next session begins by reconstructing what happened in the last one rather than building on it.

This project came about because I want to find ways to build on the insights that arise during sessions. I want to explore how to promote habits of self-directed work for things like self regulation, creativity, and awareness of how our sensory experience impacts our cognitive, emotional, and social wellbeing.

Where the idea came from

Several things converged at once. During the pandemic, when in-person sessions became impossible, I had to think quickly about how to do music therapy remotely. That process of sharing sound through a screen, collaborating on creative work at a distance, and staying genuinely connected with people without being in the same room turned out to be more generative than I expected. It changed what I thought the therapeutic relationship actually requires. It also made me think about what could happen if clients had tools they could use themselves, between sessions, and bring back into the conversation. Since then I've found ways to collaborate on compositions remotely, or give composition projects to clients that we can analyse together during our sessions, exploring the themes and how they connect back to daily life.

I was also watching the way large platforms were reshaping the therapy profession. Things like algorithmic matching and the reduction of a care relationship to something that resembles a consumer transaction. Therapists being ranked by metrics that have nothing to do with the quality of the work. I would rather focus on continuity and genuine relationship.

It's also hard to avoid the rise of people using AI as a therapist. The need for something consistent, accessible, available at any hour is real. But so are the risks of systems that lack the ethical responsibility and human presence that genuine care requires. That tension is still something I'm thinking through.

Using the lab

The Sensory Intelligence Lab is a membership platform — a home base for your ongoing mental health journey. It brings tools, creative practice, and human sessions into one place, so that everything is connected and nothing gets lost in the gaps.

For regulation and self-awareness: a regulation engine that generates soundscapes using the ISO principle, meeting your current state and guiding you toward where you want to be. Breathing exercises drawn from evidence-based practice. A daily check-in that builds a picture of your patterns over time: your energy, your mood, your sensory load, the words that keep coming up. A timed journal with soundscapes and prompts.

For training and perception: ear training, spatial awareness, and listening exercises designed to develop auditory attention and sensitivity. These are the kinds of skills that music therapists spend years cultivating, and the hope is to make them accessible.

For creative practice: composition tools and guided modules designed for people who want to make music but don't know where to start. Structured explorations of a creative process, with education about the sound and compositional techniques of a variety of different musicians, and a way to explore it yourself with no prior experience required.

For the ongoing relationship: memberships can include scheduled video sessions with me at rates designed to make sustained engagement possible. But the point is not the sessions alone. It is what connects them — the record of what you've been working on, what you've noticed, what you've made, so that we can begin each conversation from where things actually are, and build on it rather than reconstruct it.

Who this is for

People who want more consistency in their mental health practice and aren't finding it in the structures currently available. People who have had therapy and found it useful but couldn't sustain it — financially, logistically, or in terms of maintaining momentum between appointments. People who have never had formal therapy but would like a bit of structure for their own self-directed work.

People who are neurodivergent and find that standard approaches don't quite fit the way they think, process, and regulate. People who are drawn to music and creativity as ways of understanding themselves but maybe need some guidance to explore it properly. People who have a therapist and want to make more of the time between sessions. People who are curious about sound, the nervous system, and what it means to pay closer attention to inner experience.

You don't need a musical background. You just need to be interested in knowing yourself better.

The part about collaboration

This platform is very new, and the people using it are part of how it evolves. I don't think the right model for mental healthcare in this moment has been figured out yet. I have convictions about some things — the importance of the human relationship, the value of creative practice, the necessity of continuity — and I have much less certainty about others. Like the right balance between self-directed work and direct human contact, the features that make a real difference over time, the economic structures that make this sustainable for everyone, the role of AI. These are questions I'm still working through.

If you try the Lab and find something isn't working, I want to know. If something helps in a way you didn't expect, I want to know. The people who use this platform are participants in figuring out what this kind of care can be.
