Intervals

An interval is the distance in pitch between two notes, measured in semitones. The smallest interval in Western music is the minor second (one semitone — like E to F). The largest in this exercise is the octave (twelve semitones — the same note at double the frequency). In between are all the intervals that give music its characteristic tension, colour, and movement: the perfect fifth (seven semitones, stable and open), the tritone (six, unstable and dissonant), the major third (four, bright and settled).

Diatonic mode presents only the intervals found in the major scale — a more manageable set to begin with. Chromatic mode includes all twelve semitones, requiring finer discrimination. Together presentation plays both notes simultaneously, which develops harmonic hearing. Ascending and descending play the notes in sequence, which develops melodic interval recognition — a different and complementary skill.

Chords

A chord is three or more notes sounded together. The most fundamental are triads — three notes built from a root, a third, and a fifth. Major triads (root, major third, perfect fifth) are bright and stable. Minor triads (root, minor third, perfect fifth) are darker. Augmented (root, major third, augmented fifth) and diminished (root, minor third, diminished fifth) are less common but important colours.

Seventh chords add a fourth note a seventh above the root, creating richer, more complex sounds. The dominant seventh (major triad plus minor seventh) is the most important in tonal music — its tension resolves strongly to the tonic. Extended chords (ninths, elevenths, thirteenths) are the language of jazz harmony, adding layers of colour above the seventh.

Inversions change which note is at the bottom without changing the chord's identity. A C major chord in first inversion has E in the bass; in second inversion it has G in the bass. The same three notes — but the sound changes significantly. Training with inversions develops the ability to identify a chord regardless of how it is voiced, which is essential for real-world listening.

Settings guide

Fixed root always uses C as the lowest note, removing one variable and letting you focus purely on interval quality or chord type. Random root transposes to a different starting pitch each time, which is more challenging but more musically realistic. Arpeggio voicing plays chord notes one at a time, making the individual intervals more audible — useful when learning new chord types. Together presents the full sonority at once, which is closer to how chords appear in music.

Why train your ear

Ear training develops the connection between sound and understanding — the ability to hear music and know what you are hearing, not just feel it. For music therapy it has particular relevance: clients working with music often benefit from developing a more conscious relationship to the sounds they encounter, whether that means recognising familiar intervals in songs that carry emotional significance, understanding the harmonic movement in pieces they are working with, or simply building the confidence that comes from knowing what something is. The skills trained here transfer directly to listening, to instrument playing, and to the broader capacity for active musical engagement.
