Sound you can see

In the late 1950s a British composer built a machine that turned drawings into music. You painted onto strips of clear 35 mm film, the strips ran through the machine, light-sensitive cells read the marks, and what they read became pitch, loudness, vibrato and reverberation. The composer was Daphne Oram, and she called the system Oramics. It is one of the earliest instruments in which you compose by looking at time instead of writing notes.

This page is not a copy of her machine. It borrows the central idea (a picture is the score, and the picture is read from left to right) and builds a different instrument around it: five coloured inks, each with its own job in the music. The rest of this page is about the machine, the woman who made it, and what it left behind.

Daphne Oram, 1925–2003

A studio engineer who wanted to compose

Daphne Oram was born in Devizes, Wiltshire, on 31 December 1925, and went to Sherborne School for Girls in Dorset. As a child she was curious about sound beyond the keyboard; her brothers introduced her to radio electronics. In 1942 she was offered a place at the Royal College of Music. She turned it down and joined the BBC instead, as a junior studio engineer and “music balancer”.

It was wartime, and her work was practical. Among other things she “shadowed” live concerts with pre-recorded copies, ready to be switched in if an air raid cut the broadcast. After hours she taught herself what tape could do: recording, cutting, splicing, looping, changing speed and playing sound backwards. These were the same techniques that would later fill the BBC’s Radiophonic Workshop, and she was using them years before the Workshop existed.

A piece nobody played for seventy years

In the 1940s she composed Still Point, for a double orchestra, live electronics and turntables. The BBC turned it down and it was not performed in her lifetime. Its first performance was on 24 June 2016, by Shiva Feshareki and the London Contemporary Orchestra, and a revised version was played at the BBC Proms on 23 July 2018. It is a reminder of how far ahead of what was possible she often was.

The Radiophonic Workshop, and leaving it

By the 1950s Oram was pressing the BBC to take electronic music seriously, and in 1956 she put a proposal for a dedicated experimental workshop to BBC managers. In 1957 she scored Amphitryon 38 using a sine-wave oscillator, a tape recorder and filters she designed herself; the Daphne Oram Trust describes it as the first wholly electronic score in BBC history. Early in 1958 she and Desmond Briscoe set up the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, and she became its first studio manager.

She did not stay. In October 1958 the BBC sent her to the experimental music events at Expo 58 in Brussels, and she came back frustrated that the BBC would not make electronic composition a priority. She resigned later that year and, in 1959, set up her own studio: the Oramics Studios for Electronic Composition, in Tower Folly, a converted oast house at Fairseat in Kent.

Drawing as a way of composing

Oram had a problem that every composer who worked with tape shared. Tape music was made by cutting and joining recorded sound, which was slow, and the result could not be shaped note by note the way a performer shapes a phrase. She wanted a machine designed to make electronic sound directly, and she wanted the composer to control it by writing. In her own words, “every nuance, every subtlety of phrasing” had to be possible “just by a change in the written form”.

Her answer was to make the writing literal. A composer would draw on film, and the film would play the instrument. The idea has a long family tree (in the 1930s, for example, Yevgeny Sholpo’s Variophone read shapes cut into discs, and the animator Norman McLaren drew directly onto film soundtracks), but Oram’s was one of the most ambitious attempts to turn it into a complete instrument for composing.

How the machine worked

Work on the machine began in 1962, funded by a grant of £3,550 from the Calouste Gulbenkian Foundation (a further £1,000 followed in 1965). Graham Wrench, an engineer, worked with her on it in the 1960s.

In its final form, ten strips of 35 mm film ran side by side across the top of the machine and did the following:

  • Four strips for pitch. Each one stood for a digit of the frequency in hertz (ones, tens, hundreds and thousands). A spot of paint on a track switched a value on, so to ask for 440 Hz you painted a mark on the “hundreds” strip and another on the “tens”. Oram thought in frequencies rather than note names, which let her work outside the usual scales. A new pitch stayed in force until the next one was painted.
  • One strip for vibrato. A continuous drawn line pushed the pitch up or down according to its distance from the middle of the track.
  • Four strips for loudness. The machine had four tone generators, and each had its own drawn volume contour.
  • One strip for reverberation, a drawn graph of how much of the sound was sent to the reverberation room.

The timbre was drawn too, but not on the film. Each tone generator was something like an oscilloscope run in reverse. A hand-drawn waveform was held on a glass slide in front of a cathode-ray tube, and a light-sensitive tube followed its outline. The glowing dot traced the drawn shape, and the movement of that dot was the sound. Draw a smooth hill and you got a soft tone; draw a jagged one and you got a harsh one. The speed of the sweep set the pitch.

The machine played one voice at a time. To make a chord or a texture, Oram recorded one line to tape, drew the next, played it in sync and recorded again on a multitrack recorder. She described the method as painstaking, since a single note needed several drawn marks. A piece took patience. But the composer could see the entire piece laid out in front of them as a drawing, and change any part of it with a brush.

“Composers will be mingling with capacitors.”

Daphne Oram, An Individual Note of Music, Sound and Electronics, as quoted by the Daphne Oram Trust

The ideas behind it

Oram did not see electronic music as a novelty. She believed it could free composers from the limits of performance and of conventional instruments, and she was interested in how sound connects to physics, perception and even metaphysics. Her notes define Oramics as “the study of sound and its relationship to life”. In later years she divided her work into two kinds: “commercial Oramics”, the compositions for film, radio, theatre and exhibitions that paid for the research, and “mystical Oramics”, which looked into the way the ear responds to sound and into vibration.

Her book An Individual Note of Music, Sound and Electronics, published in the early 1970s, is as much philosophy as manual. She wrote about the composer Edgard Varèse’s hope for a score that would reach the listener without being altered by an interpreter. Oramics was in part a way to get there: if the drawing is the sound, there is nobody in between.

She also returned, again and again, to a passage from Francis Bacon’s New Atlantis, written in the seventeenth century: “We have also sound-houses, where we practise and demonstrate all sounds, and their generation.” A sound-house is a good description of what she was building: a place to explore every kind of sound, and how it is made.

The work

Money was always short, and Oram earned it as a working composer. She made electronic sound for radio, television, theatre, exhibitions and film: for Jack Clayton’s The Innocents (1961), for the short films Snow (1963) and Rail (1967), and, uncredited, for sounds that were used in Dr. No (1962) and the Bond films that followed. Her concert works include Four Aspects (1960) and Pulse Persephone (1965). Contrasts Essonic, dated 1963, is credited by the Daphne Oram Trust as her first composition made entirely from drawn sound.

She did not patent the machine. According to Graham Wrench, officials at the Patent Office thought it too advanced, warned that protecting it in every country would be expensive, and she decided to keep the workings quiet instead.

Later years

In the 1980s she developed a software version of Oramics for the Acorn Archimedes computer, funded by the Ralph Vaughan Williams Trust. She hoped to carry on with her “mystical Oramics” research, but without enough funding it was never fully realised. In the late 1970s she had begun an unfinished manuscript, The Sound of the Past: A Resonating Speculation, which suggests that Neolithic burial mounds, Stonehenge and the Great Pyramid may have worked as acoustic resonators. She had two strokes in the 1990s and stopped working. She died in Maidstone, Kent, on 5 January 2003, aged 77.

What she left

The only Oramics machine was acquired by the Science Museum in London with help from Goldsmiths, University of London. It was conserved and has been shown there; the museum has chosen not to run it, to protect its historic value. Her archive passed after her death to the composer Hugh Davies, then to the Sonic Arts Network, and in 2008 to Goldsmiths, where the Daphne Oram Collection is open to the public. The Oram Awards, which support innovation in sound and music, were launched in 2017 by the PRS Foundation and the New BBC Radiophonic Workshop.

Her influence is easiest to see in the instruments that followed. Systems for drawing sound include Iannis Xenakis’s UPIC (first used in the late 1970s) and many modern programs that read a picture as sound. This page belongs to the same family, and so does any app that lets you draw a shape and hear it. The deeper inheritance is an attitude: that a composer should be able to work with sound as directly as a painter works with colour.

What is different here

Oram’s machine was built for a composer with a precise sound in mind and the patience to specify it. A browser lets us change the balance. Here, a mark is heard the moment you make it, and you can undo it just as quickly. We replaced her four stacked pitch strips with height, which is closer to how most people think about drawing. We turned her tone generators into colours, because a colour is a faster way to choose a voice than a drawn waveform. We added things she did not have: a beat made of dots, chords made of stacked bars, echoes, a mirror, and a key to snap to if you want one.

What we kept is the part that matters most: you make marks in time, and then you listen to what they said.

Try this

  1. Draw a sound you remember. Pick one ink and spend two minutes drawing a sound you know well: a kettle, a train, a door. Play it. What did the picture get right, and what did it miss?
  2. Question and answer. Draw a short melody phrase. Turn on Echo ×2, then change the second copy so that it answers the first.
  3. Weather. Use only Texture and Rhythm. Scatter dots for rain, then slide a wide, soft stroke across the top for wind.
  4. One shape, five ways. Draw the same spiral in every ink. Notice what each ink makes of it.
  5. Slide the ink. Take a picture you like and use the Slide tool to move the bass a little to the left. The same music changes character as its parts fall out of step.
