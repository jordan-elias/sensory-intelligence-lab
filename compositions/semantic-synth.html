Five voices · No understanding

You are listening to a system trying to understand you

This is not an AI music generator. You are not asking a machine to compose in a style, or to express a mood, or to accompany you emotionally. What you are doing is submitting language to a computational process and listening to what that process sounds like from the inside.

When you type a sentence, a human hears meaning — association, memory, tone, weight. A language processing system does something different and stranger: it counts, ranks, clusters, predicts. It breaks the sentence into tokens and looks each one up in a statistical table. It measures the distances between words in a high-dimensional space. It computes a probability distribution over what token most likely comes next, given everything that came before. It does not understand. It computes. And in that computation — in the weights and distances and distributions — there is something that has the structure of music, even though no one intended it to be.

Each of the five voices you hear corresponds to a distinct mode of computational analysis. They run in parallel, simultaneously, the way a language model's layers run in parallel — not sequentially reading the text, but processing different aspects of it all at once. Together they form a texture that is precisely as complex as the linguistic structure of what you wrote. Not more expressive, not more emotional — exactly as structured as the language itself.

The Five Voices

Voice I — Token Pulse

The most literal layer. Each word becomes a single percussive event. Its duration is proportional to its character length. Its pitch is derived from its frequency rank in English — the most common words (the, and, a, is, of) produce the lowest tones; rare or unusual words produce high, bright clicks. Punctuation produces silence. The result is a rhythmic digital pulse that encodes the statistical texture of the vocabulary. Common language sounds low and dense; unusual, specific, or technical language sounds high and sparse. This is genuinely how a language model "sees" rarity — as surprise, as information content, as a moment that requires more processing weight.

Voice II — Probability Field

At each word in the sequence, a bigram model estimates what is likely to come next: a probability distribution over possible continuations. The five most probable next tokens are played simultaneously as sine tones, their amplitudes proportional to their probability weights. When the next word is highly predictable — when the context strongly constrains what can follow — the voice produces a clear, harmonically stable chord: one dominant tone, others near-silent. When the next word is uncertain — when many continuations are roughly equally probable — the voice produces a cloud of quiet tones, a diffuse spectral haze. You are hearing the system's confidence fluctuate moment by moment. Predictable language sounds harmonically stable. Uncertain or surprising language sounds diffuse and restless.

Voice III — Semantic Clusters

Words are mapped to semantic categories: motion, color, number, body, time, place, emotion, abstraction, relation, nature, and others. Within a sentence, the trajectory through these categories — which semantic domains the words visit and in what order — determines a harmonic field. Words in the same cluster produce unison or close-interval relationships. Semantically distant words produce wider intervals, dissonance, spread. A sentence that stays within one semantic domain sounds harmonically coherent. A sentence that jumps between distant domains — technical vocabulary alongside emotional language, abstract concepts alongside concrete physical description — sounds wide, dissonant, unstable. This is measuring something real about semantic coherence: how much a piece of language is about one thing versus many things simultaneously.

Voice IV — Syntactic Architecture

Grammatical function maps directly to register. Nouns anchor a slow, sustained bass. Verbs produce mid-register accents. Adjectives color the high register. Adverbs produce brief high inflections. Conjunctions and prepositions produce transitions. Sentence-final punctuation produces a slow decay, a breath, a pause in the structure. What you hear is the grammatical skeleton of the sentence rendered as register and rhythm. A noun-heavy sentence sounds weighted, sustained, low. A verb-heavy sentence sounds active, mid-register, kinetic. Complex nested clauses produce layered sustained tones that accumulate and slowly resolve. The grammar has a sonic shape that is not arbitrary — it corresponds to real structural properties of the language.

Voice V — Entropy Drone

This voice never stops once activated. It computes the running type-token ratio — the ratio of unique words to total words — and the Shannon entropy of the word distribution in everything submitted so far. These are mapped to a slowly evolving ambient texture: a continuous tone whose pitch and spectral content shift with the complexity and variety of the accumulated language. Highly repetitive text produces a low, simple, stable tone. High-entropy, varied, lexically rich text produces a higher, more spectrally complex texture. When you stop submitting, it decays slowly back toward silence. It is the system's background hum — its internal state, its running sense of how much it has been asked to process and how varied that processing has been.

George Lewis — Responsive Systems

A system that listens

George Lewis is a composer, trombonist, and scholar whose work over the past fifty years has consistently asked what it would mean for a machine not just to produce sound but to listen — to develop a response to what it has heard, to build a history of interaction, to change its behavior based on the accumulated record of a relationship. His composition Voyager (1986–ongoing) is the clearest instantiation of this question: a software system that improvises in real time with human musicians, analyzing their pitch, rhythm, density, and articulation, and generating responses that are neither random nor predetermined. Voyager has a memory, a set of analytical dispositions, a tendency to reinforce or destabilize patterns depending on what it detects in its human partners.

Lewis situates this work explicitly within an African American improvisational tradition — the call-and-response structures of blues, jazz, and gospel — while simultaneously engaging with the European tradition of algorithmic composition. The machine is not a neutral tool; it has aesthetic preferences, embedded in its code, that reflect choices made by its composer. When Voyager destabilizes a pattern, that is a compositional decision. When it reinforces one, that is also a decision. The system has a point of view.

Semantic Synth inherits this disposition. After each submission, the system builds a fingerprint of your language: its rarity profile, semantic range, syntactic complexity, predictability, and entropy level. It stores these across your session and compares each new input against this accumulated history. Similar language stabilizes the system — voices settle, patterns lock, the probability field narrows. Different language disturbs it — voices spread, patterns fragment, the harmonic field widens. You are not expressing emotion; the system is not responding to meaning. But you are in a structural relationship with it, and that relationship develops over time.

Ryoji Ikeda — Data as Material

The beauty of unmediated data

Ryoji Ikeda's practice begins with a refusal: he will not translate data into emotion, will not use information as raw material to be shaped into something warmer and more human. In works like test pattern (2008–ongoing) and data.tron (2007), data flows through his systems and is rendered directly — as light, as sound, as the visual pattern of binary information at the speed of transmission. The aesthetic does not soften or humanize; it reveals structure, precision, the intrinsic form of information as it actually exists.

The claim underlying this work is significant: that data has its own beauty, and that this beauty is obscured rather than revealed when we insist on mediating it through emotional or narrative frames. A frequency distribution is not a metaphor for something else; it is itself. The rhythm produced by word length is not an approximation of a drum pattern — it is a sonic rendering of a measurable property of language. Ikeda insists on the literalness of the mapping, the directness of the correspondence, the refusal of expressivity in favor of structural honesty.

I am not interested in emotion. I am interested in the accuracy of the data.

— Ryoji Ikeda

The token pulse voice in Semantic Synth is the most Ikeda-like: each word produces exactly one sound, whose pitch and duration are determined by measurable lexical properties. No interpolation, no smoothing, no emotional shaping. The word the produces a low, brief click because it is the most common word in English and it is short. The word phosphorescent produces a high, sustained tone because it is rare and long. This is not poetic. It is accurate.

What Language Models Actually Do

Prediction without understanding

A large language model does not understand language in the way you understand language. It has no intention, no referential experience, no memory of having learned what words mean from the world. What it has is a very large table of statistical relationships between tokens, learned from an enormous corpus of text, which allows it to predict — with remarkable accuracy — what token is likely to come next given a sequence of previous tokens.

This is genuinely strange and worth sitting with. The model has never seen a chair. It has processed, during training, millions of sentences that contain the word chair in proximity to words like sit, table, wooden, comfortable, leg. From this, it has built a dense web of statistical associations that allows it to use the word chair correctly in almost any context. But there is no chair in the model. There is only the pattern of the word chair in relation to other words.

When a language model processes a sentence, it runs it through many layers of attention — a mechanism that computes, for each word, how much it should influence the processing of every other word in the sequence. The word bank in "I sat by the bank" attends strongly to "river" if that word is present; the same word in "I deposited money at the bank" attends strongly to "deposited" and "money." These attention patterns are not programmed; they emerge from training. They are the model's learned sense of which words are relevant to which other words in which contexts.

The probability field voice makes this process partially audible. The bigram model embedded in this piece is incomparably simpler than a real language model — a two-word window rather than thousands of tokens of context, a small corpus rather than the breadth of human written language. But the structure is the same: at each moment, a distribution over possible continuations, with some possibilities weighted heavily and others nearly excluded. When you hear the probability field produce a clear chord, you are hearing the system's near-certainty about what comes next. When you hear a diffuse cloud of tones, you are hearing its uncertainty — the moment when many continuations are roughly equally probable, when the language has arrived at a point of high branching factor.

The philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote that if a lion could speak, we could not understand it — because understanding requires shared forms of life, shared practices, a world held in common. A language model can speak with remarkable fluency, and we can understand its words. But its relationship to those words is nothing like ours. It has no form of life. It has distributions. And those distributions, made audible, are what you have been hearing.
