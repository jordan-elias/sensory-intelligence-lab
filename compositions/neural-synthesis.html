How it works

Neural Synthesis models a small network of sound-generating and signal-processing nodes connected by weighted edges. When the network runs, each node sums its incoming signals, passes them through a nonlinear activation function, and emits an audio output that flows to neighboring nodes. No sound is pre-composed — it emerges from the interaction of the system with itself.

The design follows David Tudor's approach in works like Rainforest: circuits that listen to each other, feeding back, accumulating instability, then resolving into unexpected textures. Paired with Hebbian learning — "neurons that fire together wire together" — the network's behavior slowly changes over time as active connections strengthen and quiet ones fade.

Node types

The network contains four kinds of nodes. Oscillator nodes generate continuous tones — sine, triangle, or chaotic waveforms — whose frequency drifts slowly if drift is enabled. Filter nodes shape incoming signals with low-pass or bandpass filters, carving the spectrum. Nonlinear nodes apply functions like tanh, wave-folding, or sigmoid to their input, introducing harmonic complexity and instability. Delay nodes hold signal in memory, creating echoes, rhythmic patterns, and phase relationships between distant parts of the network.

Temperature

The temperature slider is the primary expressive control. At low values the network settles into stable drones — slow, organ-like tones reminiscent of Eliane Radigue's long-form works. At high values feedback accumulates faster, nonlinear nodes begin to saturate, and the system produces chaotic bursts and unstable resonance. The sweet spot around 40–60% produces slow evolution with occasional turbulence.

Injecting energy

Click any node in the visualization to inject a pulse of energy directly into it. The network responds — neighboring nodes light up, connections glow, and the sonic texture shifts. This is the most immediate way to disturb the system and hear it recover. Try clicking the same node repeatedly to force a feedback loop, or click different nodes to set multiple regions of the network into activity.

Mutate and rewire

Press ↯ mutate to randomly shift node frequencies, weights, and activation functions — producing a new sonic species without stopping the network. Press ⇄ rewire to randomly add and remove connections, changing the network's topology while it runs. Together these tools let you evolve the system continuously, never returning to the same configuration twice.

Topology modes

The topology selector changes how nodes are connected when you reset. Random connects nodes at chance, producing unpredictable behavior. Ring arranges nodes in a loop — signal circulates continuously. Cluster creates two groups with sparse inter-group connections, producing two interacting ecosystems. Chain passes signal through nodes in sequence, like a physical room with multiple chambers.

Tudor and feedback

David Tudor spent decades building electronic instruments from scratch — not instruments in the traditional sense, but networks of circuits, each one feeding signal into the next, listening and responding in ways no single hand could predict or control. Works like Rainforest (1968) distributed transducers across physical objects — metal sheets, ceramic bowls, wooden planks — letting each surface shape and color sound according to its own resonant character. Tudor's instruments had no score, no fixed output. They had behavior.

The key concept is feedback: the output of a system becomes its input. In electronics, feedback can stabilize, sustain, or push a signal into runaway oscillation. It can produce rich harmonic content from nothing, or collapse a structured signal into noise. Tudor embraced this indeterminacy. He didn't ask what the circuit would produce — he asked what conditions would allow the circuit to become interesting, and then listened. The performer's role shifted from expression to cultivation: setting parameters, introducing perturbations, then stepping back as the system found its own behavior.

Neural Synthesis inherits this disposition. The temperature and feedback controls are not mix parameters — they are ecological conditions. You set the climate; the network decides what grows.

Neural networks

A neural network, in its most abstract form, is a graph: a set of nodes and the weighted connections between them. Each node receives signals from its neighbors, combines them, and produces a new output. The combination is shaped by a weight — a number that scales how strongly one node influences another. Positive weights amplify; negative weights suppress; weights near zero allow signals to pass but barely notice them.

The output of a node is not simply the sum of its inputs. It passes through an activation function — a mathematical transform that introduces nonlinearity. Without nonlinearity, a network of any depth reduces to a single linear equation; there is no complexity, no emergence. Activation functions like tanh, sigmoid, and wave-folding bend and saturate the signal, enabling a small network to represent vastly more complex relationships than its size would suggest. They are the origin of the harmonic richness you hear as nodes interact: two pure sine waves entering a tanh function produce a spectrum that neither wave contained on its own.

In machine learning, weights are adjusted through training — repeated exposure to examples, with errors propagated backward through the graph. In this instrument, weights evolve through Hebbian learning: nodes that activate together strengthen their connection. There is no target, no error signal, no optimization toward a goal. The network learns only what it does — a self-referential loop that slowly sculpts its own behavior through the act of running.

The brain

The brain is, at its substrate, a network: roughly 86 billion neurons, each connected to thousands of others, exchanging electrochemical signals across synapses whose strength changes with activity. What makes it remarkable is not the complexity of any individual neuron — a single cell is relatively simple — but the patterns of coordinated activity that emerge across millions of them simultaneously. Consciousness, perception, memory: none of these live in a single place. They are processes, distributed across the network's behavior in time.

One of the most persistent features of brain activity is oscillation. Neurons across distant regions synchronize their firing into rhythmic waves — alpha, beta, theta, gamma — at frequencies that shift with attention, sleep, emotion, and cognitive load. These oscillations are not noise; they are the brain's coordination mechanism. When two regions need to communicate, they synchronize. When they need to decouple, their oscillations drift apart. The coupling between oscillators — the degree to which one region's rhythm entrains another's — is a fundamental computational operation of the nervous system.

Emergence is what happens when local interactions produce global behavior that no individual element contains or could predict. A single neuron does not hear; billions of them, organized into the right patterns of connectivity and timing, produce auditory experience. In Neural Synthesis, individual oscillator nodes are trivial — a sine wave at a harmonic frequency. But as connections form, weights evolve, and feedback propagates through delay and nonlinear transformation, textures arise that none of the nodes produce alone. This is not a metaphor for neural emergence. It is the same phenomenon at a different scale: local rules, global behavior, and the irreducible surprise of a system listening to itself.
