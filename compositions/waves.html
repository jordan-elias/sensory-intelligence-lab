About Éliane Radigue

Éliane Radigue (1932–2024) was a pioneer of electronic music who worked with the ARP 2500 synthesizer in the 1970s and 1980s. Rather than working with conventional synthesis techniques, she used the instrument to create slowly evolving, hypnotic drones — pieces that could last 20, 30, or even 40 minutes with imperceptible changes accumulating across the duration. She believed in patience, in listening deeply to tiny variations, in music as a meditation.

How this tool works

Three sine wave oscillators run at slightly different frequencies. Because they're not perfectly in tune, they create "beating" patterns at their difference frequencies — you'll hear this as a slow wavering or shimmering effect. Each oscillator also has its own LFO (low-frequency oscillator) modulating its frequency slightly, causing the beating pattern to evolve over time.

The drift rate controls how quickly the LFOs change the frequencies. Lower drift = very slow, almost imperceptible change. Higher drift = faster, more audible morphing.

Individual oscillator control

You now have complete control over each oscillator's frequency and volume. This allows you to create asymmetric relationships, explore specific intervals, or emphasize particular oscillators in the mix. Try setting one oscillator much lower in pitch and volume for a subtle undertone, or create tight frequency clusters for dense beating patterns.

Listening techniques

Low volume: Play the drone very quietly. Many of Radigue's pieces are only fully appreciated at soft levels — tiny variations become audible when there's less absolute loudness.

Deep listening: Let the piece play for 5–10 minutes before deciding what you're hearing. The changes are gradual; it takes time for patterns to emerge.

Find interference patterns: Listen for the rhythmic pulsing created by the oscillators interfering with each other. As drift changes the frequencies, these patterns shift and evolve.

Parameter guide

Oscillator Frequency: The fundamental pitch of each oscillator. Lower frequencies (40–100 Hz) create deep, meditative drones; higher frequencies (150–300 Hz) produce brighter, more tense pieces. Experiment with intervals: perfect fifths (3:2 ratio), octaves (2:1), or very close frequencies (within 1–5 Hz) for pronounced beating.

Oscillator Volume: Individual volume control for each oscillator. Equal volumes create balanced interference; unequal volumes can emphasize certain pitches or create subtle undertones.

Drift Rate: How quickly the LFOs modulate. Ultra-low (0.01–0.03) feels almost static. Medium (0.05–0.1) feels alive. High (0.2+) feels quite active.

Noise Floor: A small amount of pink noise adds texture and prevents the piece from sounding too "synthetic." Try very small amounts (off to low).

Creating your own composition

Experiment with combinations: tight frequency clusters with slow drift create hypnotic, pulsing textures. Wide intervals with faster drift feel more exploratory. Try detuning oscillators by exact intervals (perfect fifth = multiply base by 1.5, major third = multiply by 1.25) or by very small amounts (0.5–2 Hz) for slow beating. Save your favorite settings as presets for later exploration.

Download & use

Generate a 5, 10, or 20-minute version. These pieces work well as ambient background, as material for further sound design, or as a basis for improvisation or layering with other sounds.
