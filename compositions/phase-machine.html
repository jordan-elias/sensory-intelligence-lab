How it works

Two identical copies of the same loop play simultaneously. Tape A runs at normal speed. Tape B runs slightly faster — the percentage set by the drift slider. As they play, the offset between them gradually increases, creating subtle rhythmic and melodic transformations.

Use any of the three sources: the built-in default loop (a short rhythmic pattern designed to make phasing effects immediately audible), an uploaded audio file in any browser-supported format, or record your own voice or instrument through your microphone.

Waveform editor

When you upload a file or record audio, a waveform editor appears. Drag the handles at either end of the waveform to select the region you want to use as your loop. The selection shows in real-time with start time, end time, and duration. Click "Preview Selection" to hear just that portion, or "Use Selection" to crop the audio to that range. "Reset to Full" restores the original uncropped audio.

Sync hold

Hold the K key on your keyboard (or press and hold the K button on mobile) to momentarily run both tapes at the same rate. While held, the offset freezes. Release to resume drift from wherever you held it.

The original phasing effect in Reich's tape pieces was an accident: two tape machines running at slightly different motor speeds. The sync hold inverts that — it introduces control into an otherwise autonomous process.

What to listen for

As the loops drift, the auditory system actively constructs patterns that don't exist in either loop alone. At the mid-phase position (offset ≈ 50% of loop length), the material is most transformed. Short loops phase rapidly; longer loops evolve slowly across minutes.

Loop length matters

Shorter loops phase faster and produce more rapid rhythmic changes. Longer loops drift slowly, with transformations taking several minutes to complete. Try the same drift rate with recordings of different lengths to hear how duration affects the perceptual outcome.

Download phased output

Generate a WAV file of the phased result. Choose your duration (1-5 minutes) and click Download. The file will contain both tapes phasing according to your current drift rate setting.

Keyboard shortcuts

Press Space to play/pause. Hold K to engage sync hold.

It's Gonna Rain

The piece that started it was called It's Gonna Rain. In 1964, Steve Reich encountered a Pentecostal preacher named Brother Walter in Union Square, San Francisco, and recorded his sermon on a portable machine. He extracted a single phrase — "it's gonna rain" — and looped it on two separate tape machines that were meant to run in unison but were slightly off, one running a little faster than the other.

Over the next several minutes the loops drifted apart and gradually back together, generating a continuous process of rhythmic effects: canons, syncopations, moments of sudden unison, dense polyrhythm as the tapes slowly converged again. What had been a mechanical inconsistency became a compositional method. In later works like Piano Phase (1967) and Violin Phase (1967), Reich transferred the process to live performers — two pianists or two violinists playing the same repeating figure, with one player instructed to gradually accelerate until a new phase relationship locked in.

What the ear does with drift

When two identical loops run slightly out of phase, something unexpected happens in the auditory system: melodies, rhythms, and patterns emerge that are not present in either loop individually. The brain does not hear two separate streams so much as actively group and regroup the incoming material, finding structure in the relationship between the two signals — structure that exists only in their perceptual interaction.

This is a vivid demonstration of what Albert Bregman called auditory scene analysis: the brain's continuous work of organising acoustic input into coherent objects. In phasing, that work produces emergent melodic patterns, apparent accelerations and decelerations, moments where the two streams seem to fuse into one and moments where they pull clearly apart. None of these are composed. The perceptual effects are most pronounced around the mid-phase position — when the offset is roughly half the loop length — where the material from the two loops is most interleaved and the auditory system has the most work to do.

Music as a gradual process

Reich's 1968 essay Music as a Gradual Process articulates something central to how this machine is meant to be used: the listener's engagement is not passive reception but active participation. The slow perceptual tracking of a change that is too gradual to pinpoint but too large to miss. You cannot hear the moment the loops drift, but you can hear that they have drifted. The experience is one of continuous presence with something that is always already different from how it was.

The sync hold — the K key — is not present in most digital implementations of phasing. Without it, a phase machine can only simulate the original tape accident: continuous, uncontrollable drift. Reich's live performance practice made room for holding a phase position rather than endlessly moving through them. The K key returns that choice to the listener.

On attending to process

Change is rarely visible at the moment it occurs. What becomes apparent is often retrospective — a sense that something is now different, that a pattern has shifted, that a position once held with great force is now held more lightly. The phasing process is a model for this kind of attention: not watching for a specific event, but staying present with something that is continuously, almost imperceptibly, becoming otherwise.
