The cocktail party problem

The term was coined by Colin Cherry in 1953 to describe the auditory system's remarkable ability to selectively attend to a single voice or sound source within a complex acoustic scene. At a crowded party, you can focus on one conversation while filtering out others — not by reducing the volume of competing sounds, but by directing attentional resources toward specific auditory streams.

This capacity depends on a network of auditory and prefrontal cortex regions that work together to segregate sound sources using spatial cues (where the sound comes from), spectral cues (its frequency content), and temporal cues (its rhythmic and timing patterns). As you progress through the levels, the signal-to-noise ratio decreases and additional competing sound sources are added — requiring increasing reliance on selective attention rather than simple loudness detection.

Training on this task is associated with improved performance in real-world noisy environments, and is used in auditory rehabilitation contexts for hearing loss and auditory processing difficulties.
